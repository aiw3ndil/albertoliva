One of the most frequent questions people asked me before moving to Finland was: "Isn't it super expensive to live there?" Now, after several years living in Helsinki, I can answer with hard numbers. I'm going to share my actual monthly expenses so you get a transparent idea of what living here really costs.

<!--more-->

## My Monthly Expense Breakdown

Here are my fixed and variable expenses each month:

| Category | Monthly Cost |
|----------|--------------|
| **Rent** | €592 |
| **Electricity** | €24 |
| **Water** | €10 |
| **Mobile Phone** | €24 |
| **Transport** | €72 |
| **Insurance** | €10 |
| **Groceries** | €240 |
| **TOTAL** | **€972** |

Yes, you read that correctly. With **less than €1,000 a month**, I cover all my basic living essentials in Finland.

## Breaking Down the Numbers

### Rent: €592
This is by far the largest line item. I live in a one-room studio apartment (yksiö) in Helsinki. It's not in the dead center, but it's well within easy commuting distance. It's comfortable, modern, and very well connected.

For Helsinki standards, the price is reasonable. In the absolute city center, you could easily pay €800–€1,000 for something similar, while outside the capital, in cities like Tampere or Oulu, you could find studios for €400–€500.

### Electricity: €24
This varies slightly depending on the season. During winter, with such short days, consumption creeps up a little. But overall, Finnish appliances and heating systems are exceptionally energy-efficient, and buildings are insulated to the highest standard.

### Water: €10
Finland has some of the cleanest, highest-quality tap water in the world. You never need bottled water here. This €10 fee covers both freshwater and sewage, which is a standard flat monthly charge.

### Mobile: €24
I have a plan with unlimited high-speed data and calls. Coverage across Finland is remarkable—4G (and increasingly 5G) works seamlessly even deep in the forest.

### Transport: €72
I use the monthly pass for Helsinki regional public transport (HSL). It grants unlimited travel across the metropolitan area: metro, tram, buses, commuter trains, and even ferries to nearby islands like Suomenlinna. The network is punctual, clean, and reliable.

If I lived outside Helsinki or owned a car, transportation costs would be different. But for city living, public transit is unequivocally the best choice.

### Home Insurance: €10
This is basic renter's home insurance covering personal contents and liability. Most tenancy agreements in Finland require it by law, and it provides total peace of mind.

### Groceries: €240
This is the most flexible category where personal habits matter most. I shop predominantly at supermarkets like Lidl, Prisma, and S-Market. I cook almost all my meals at home, which keeps the grocery budget under tight control.

If you dine out frequently, expenses spike quickly. A standard weekday lunch special costs around €10–€15, while dinner at a sit-down restaurant easily runs €20–€40 or more per person.

## What is NOT Included

This budget covers core essentials, but does not account for discretionary or occasional spending:

- **Leisure and entertainment:** cinema, gigs, personal hobbies (€50–€100)
- **Clothing and footwear:** especially winter gear (variable)
- **Personal care and toiletries:** shampoo, household cleaning (€20–€30)
- **Social life:** coffee shops, going out with friends (€50–€100)
- **Travel:** domestic weekend trips or international travel (variable)

Factoring in these extras, a realistic budget for living comfortably in Finland sits around **€1,200–€1,500 per month**.

## Compared to Spain

Many ask if Finland is drastically more expensive than Spain. The short answer: it depends.

- **Rent** in Helsinki is comparable to—or even cheaper than—Madrid or Barcelona.
- **Groceries** are pricier, particularly fresh produce, fruits, and vegetables.
- **Public transit** is slightly more expensive, but vastly cleaner and more reliable.
- **Utilities and telecoms** (electricity, water, mobile data) are comparable or even cheaper.

The decisive factor lies in **salaries**. In Finland, even entry-level and service jobs provide dignified wages that allow you to live decently. The de facto entry-level net earnings hover around €1,500–€2,000, while median professional salaries sit around €2,500–€3,500 net.

## Conclusion

Living in Finland is not as prohibitively expensive as many assume. With conscious budgeting, **less than €1,000 covers your foundations**, and €1,500 allows you to live with comfort and occasional treats.

The secret comes down to:
- **Cooking at home** (the single greatest saving lever)
- **Relying on public transport** (no car needed in the city)
- **Leveraging public libraries and civic amenities** (Finland has an outstanding public culture)
- **Buying winter clothing during seasonal sales or second-hand** (flea markets and vintage shops are huge here)

While certain everyday items cost more than in Southern Europe, the high quality of life, dependable public infrastructure, and sheer peace of mind more than make up for the difference.
