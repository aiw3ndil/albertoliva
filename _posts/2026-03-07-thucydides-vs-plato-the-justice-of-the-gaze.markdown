In *Daybreak (Morgenröte)*, Nietzsche poses a provocative question that dismantles our reflexive tendency to romanticize abstract thought: *«What is it that I admire in Thucydides and makes me value him more than Plato?»*

This is no mere personal historical affinity; it is an uncompromising declaration of principles regarding how we ought to observe mankind and the world.

<!--more-->

### Disinterested Delight in Reality

Contrasted with Plato’s gaze—which constantly chases the moral ideal, the prescriptive «ought», or the metaphysical perfection of the Forms—Thucydides derives a far more acute, disinterested delight from flesh-and-blood human beings and occurrences that embody distinct character types. He refuses to pass judgment on whether a person is virtuous or wicked according to an externally imposed morality; instead, he seeks to uncover the precise measure of **common sense** and realism dwelling within each human temperament.

Thucydides is the keen observer who does not require reality to be other than it is in order to appreciate it. His perspective is never that of the moral judge, but that of the rigorous anatomist of history.

### The Justice of Not Slandering

Nietzsche highlights in Thucydides an uncommon «practical justice», distinctly superior to that of Plato. It is an integrity born not from codified statutes, but from unyielding intellectual honesty: **he neither slanders nor devalues men who displease him or who have brought him personal misfortune in life.**

How demanding it is to preserve such composure! Our ordinary human instinct is to diminish those who wound us or discount the virtues of those whose views we despise. Thucydides, by contrast, elevates and introduces an element of grandeur into everyone by discerning them as «types». By elevating the singular person to an archetypal category, he confers upon them a universal and lasting dignity.

### A Beacon for Posterity

This lens on human affairs is the rare heritage Thucydides bequeathed to posterity. Nietzsche bids us emulate this exact disposition: to gaze with practical justice upon that which does not fit neatly into an archetype—that which is purely accidental, individual, and flawed—treating it with the same sober respect and depth.

In an age where slander, cancel culture, and the reflexive belittling of the «other» have become default currency, the gaze of Thucydides—championed by Nietzsche—stands out as an invaluable sanctuary of intellectual courage and moral elevation.

> *"He adds and introduces something grand into all things and people, by seeing in them nothing but types."*
> — *Friedrich Nietzsche (Daybreak)*
